## Disease Is Not a Snapshot

If we step back and look at what we’ve built so far, something becomes clear.

We are dealing with a system we cannot fully see.  
And what we do see are only fragments.

So the question shifts.

Not just _what are we observing?_  
But _what is the system actually doing underneath?_

---

### From Observations to State

In practice, we work with what we can measure:

- blood tests
    
- imaging
    
- symptoms
    

But we already know these don’t fully explain what is happening.

So what are we really trying to understand?

Not the measurements themselves.

But the condition of the system that produces them.

---

### Thinking in Terms of State

At any given moment, the immune system has an internal condition.

We may describe parts of it as:

- level of activation
    
- degree of regulation
    
- extent of tissue involvement
    
- overall stability or instability
    

These are not directly measurable.

But they shape everything we observe.

And importantly — this is not a fixed list.

These are just examples of dimensions that together define the system’s state.  
Other aspects — accumulated stress, prior damage, immune memory — may also be part of it.

So rather than a single variable, we are dealing with a **space of possible states**.

---

### State Is Not Static

Once we think this way, another shift follows.

The state is not fixed.

It changes.

Sometimes gradually.  
Sometimes abruptly.

A flare is not just an event.  
It is the system moving into a different region of its state.

Remission is not just the absence of signals.  
It is another configuration — often more stable, sometimes only temporarily so.

So disease is not something we capture at a moment.

It is something that unfolds.

A **trajectory**.

---

### Why Trajectories Matter

This is where things start to look different.

A single measurement gives us a snapshot.

But what matters is the direction:

- is the system stabilizing?
    
- drifting?
    
- close to a transition?
    

Two patients may look identical at one point.

But if their trajectories differ, their futures will not.

And that is what we are actually trying to understand — often without saying it.

---

### Connecting State and Observation

If the internal state is hidden, then everything we measure is generated from it.

That creates a constraint.

Different internal states can produce similar observations.  
And similar observations can arise from very different underlying states.

This is something we see all the time.

The same CRP value in two patients — and yet, they behave completely differently.  
Or the same patient, same numbers — but a different clinical course.

What we observe does not uniquely define the state.

It only narrows the possibilities.

Which means interpretation is unavoidable.

We are not reading the system directly.

We are inferring the most plausible state that could produce what we see.

---

### Interventions Change the Trajectory

Now bring therapy into this picture.

We often think of treatment as acting on specific components.

But in reality, we are nudging the system itself.

We are trying to shift it:

- from instability toward stability
    
- from activation toward regulation
    
- from one trajectory to another
    

And the outcome depends on where the system already is.

The same intervention, applied in different states, can produce very different effects.

We’ve all seen that.

---

### A More Structured Way to Think

If we put this together, a pattern emerges:

- there is a hidden state
    
- the state evolves over time
    
- we observe indirect signals
    
- we intervene, and the system responds
    

This is not just a collection of ideas.

It is a structure.

A way of describing how the system behaves.

Often referred to — more formally — as a **state-space view** of the system.

We don’t need the formalism yet.

The intuition is enough.

---

### Where This Leaves Us

We may not be able to see the system directly.

But we can begin to understand how it moves.

And once we start thinking in terms of movement — of trajectories — something shifts.

We stop asking only:

_What does this number mean?_

And start asking:

_Where is the system, and where is it going?_

That is a very different kind of question.

And it opens the door to a very different kind of understanding.

---
## Core Idea

Disease is not a static condition but a trajectory of a system moving through hidden states over time.  
Understanding disease requires thinking in terms of evolving system state rather than isolated measurements.
